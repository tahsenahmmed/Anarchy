Neutron Stars: Matter at the Edge of Physics

Explore how massive stars can leave behind compact remnants with extraordinary density, gravity, and magnetic fields.

Anarchy Learning Notes · 8 min read

How do neutron stars form?

A massive star produces energy by nuclear fusion during much of its life. When its core can no longer support itself against gravity, the core may collapse. In a suitable mass range, the collapse creates a neutron star, while the outer layers can be expelled in a supernova explosion.

If the remnant core is too massive to be supported by neutron-star matter, it may instead collapse into a black hole. The exact boundary depends on the physics of dense matter and the remnant's rotation and thermal state.

Extreme density

A typical neutron star has a mass greater than that of the Sun compressed into a sphere roughly 20–25 kilometers across. A small amount of its matter would have an enormous mass by everyday standards. The surface gravity is also immense, and the star's escape velocity is a substantial fraction of the speed of light.

The name reflects the role of neutron-rich matter in its interior, but a neutron star is not simply a giant solid ball of neutrons. Its crust and core contain matter in different physical states, and the deepest interior remains an active research topic.

Pulsars and magnetars

A pulsar is a rotating neutron star whose beams of radiation sweep across our line of sight, producing regular pulses. The pulses are similar to the effect of a lighthouse, although the radiation mechanism is different.

Magnetars are neutron stars with exceptionally strong magnetic fields. Changes in their magnetic fields can power energetic bursts of X-rays and gamma rays.

Binary neutron stars

Some neutron stars orbit another neutron star. Gravitational waves carry energy away from such a binary, gradually shrinking its orbit. When the stars finally merge, the event can produce gravitational waves and electromagnetic emission, and may create heavy elements through rapid neutron capture.

The 2017 observation of a neutron-star merger, detected in gravitational waves and across the electromagnetic spectrum, provided strong evidence linking these mergers to the production of some heavy elements.

Quick revision

  • Neutron stars form from the collapsed cores of certain massive stars.
  • They have roughly stellar mass packed into a city-sized sphere.
  • Pulsars are observed through regular pulses from rotating neutron stars.
  • Magnetars have exceptionally strong magnetic fields.
  • Binary neutron-star mergers produce gravitational waves and can synthesize heavy elements.
Key takeawayA neutron star is the compact remnant of a massive star's core after a supernova. It packs roughly stellar mass into a sphere only about city-sized, making it one of the most extreme objects observable in the universe.
