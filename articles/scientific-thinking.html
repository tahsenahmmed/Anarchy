Scientific Thinking: How to Question an Idea

Learn how evidence, testable predictions, and careful reasoning help us distinguish strong explanations from unsupported claims.

Anarchy Learning Notes · 6 min read

Start with observation

Scientific investigations often begin with a question about something observable. A useful question is specific enough to investigate and clear enough that different people can understand what is being tested.

Build a testable hypothesis

A hypothesis is a proposed explanation that can be evaluated through observations or experiments. A strong hypothesis generates predictions: if the explanation is correct, certain results should be more likely than others.

For example, a researcher might predict that plants exposed to more light will grow faster when other important conditions are kept similar. The prediction can then be tested by measuring growth under controlled conditions.

Test, analyze, and revise

Good investigations define how variables will be measured, use appropriate comparisons, and consider alternative explanations. Repeated measurements and independent replication help reveal whether a result is reliable or may have arisen by chance or error.

Scientific conclusions are proportional to the available evidence. A result may support a hypothesis without proving it beyond all possible doubt. New evidence can lead researchers to refine or replace an explanation.

Correlation is not necessarily causation

Two variables may change together without one directly causing the other. A third factor may influence both, or the relationship may be coincidental. Establishing causation usually requires additional evidence, a plausible mechanism, and a study design that rules out competing explanations as well as possible.

Ask these questionsWhat evidence supports the claim? Could another explanation fit the data? How was the measurement made? Can other researchers reproduce the result?

Quick revision

  • Observations help generate research questions.
  • Hypotheses should generate testable predictions.
  • Controlled comparisons help evaluate explanations.
  • Correlation alone does not establish causation.
  • Scientific knowledge is strengthened through evidence, criticism, and replication.
Key takeawayScience is not simply a collection of facts. It is a systematic way to build, test, challenge, and improve explanations using evidence.
